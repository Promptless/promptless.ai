## Overview

When leadership asks what the Docs function is worth, you need numbers you can defend. Much of that story already lives in Promptless and in your Git history: doc updates drafted, reviewed, and merged as the product changes. This guide shows which signals to track, where to read each one, and how to assemble them into a case for spend or headcount.

If you maintain an open-source project, you may have no manager to report to. The same numbers then make the case to a foundation, a sponsor, or your community. They can also justify the hours you put in.

<Aside type="note">
  Promptless doesn't include a built-in reporting dashboard or a metrics export. The signals below come from places you already use, and you assemble the report yourself. Track the few numbers that matter to your leadership.
</Aside>

## Find the signals Promptless records

Three places hold the raw signal, and each answers a different question about the value Promptless adds:

- **Suggestions list.** Every draft Promptless creates, sorted into **Ready**, **PR Open**, and **Closed**, with an estimated review time for each suggestion. This shows drafting volume and review load. Filter by **Collection** to attribute activity to one doc collection, or by **Trigger source** to see which signals produce the most drafts.
- **Triggers page.** Every event Promptless reacted to. The **Suggestion Activity** filter splits events into **Suggestion Activity** and **No Suggestion Activity**. This shows coverage: how many of your product's changes Promptless caught, and how well it skips changes that don't need docs. The **From** and **To** date fields scope a specific range, and the summary line shows how many triggers match.
- **Your Git history.** Merged documentation PRs are the ground truth for shipped work, because a merged Promptless PR is a doc change that went live. Each PR's author, reviewers, and timestamps live in GitHub or GitLab. Promptless opens these PRs under its own account, so filter to that author to isolate its contribution. Read the exact account name off any docs PR Promptless has opened in your repo.

## Choose the metrics to track

Pick the few that answer the question your leadership is asking:

- **Doc updates shipped.** Count merged Promptless PRs over a period. This is the cleanest measure of output: work that reached readers. Read it from your Git history, filtered to the Promptless author.
- **Share of doc changes automated.** Compare merged Promptless PRs with all merged doc PRs in the same repo over the same period. The share answers "how much of the docs work is Promptless carrying?"
- **Coverage of product changes.** On the Triggers page, set the **From** and **To** dates. Set **Suggestion Activity** to **Suggestion Activity** and note the count in the summary line, then repeat with **No Suggestion Activity**. Comparing the two counts shows how much of your product's movement Promptless catches.
- **Review throughput and load.** Use the Suggestions list buckets and review-time estimates to show how much review the queue represents and how quickly your team clears it. This is the writing effort Promptless turns into review effort.
- **Time saved.** Multiply doc updates shipped by your team's own estimate of the hours a comparable manual update takes. Promptless doesn't measure this for you, so state your assumption plainly. A defensible estimate lands better than a precise-looking number no one can source.

<Aside type="caution">
  These counts describe activity and output, not documentation quality. Pair them with the outcome signals in the next section, so the story covers outcomes as well as volume.
</Aside>

## Pair output with outcomes

The metrics above count what Promptless produces. To show whether that output helped readers or reduced cost, pair it with outcome signals from tools your team already runs. Promptless doesn't produce, measure, or export these numbers. Read them in your own analytics, support, and docs-platform tools. The same signals also show what to improve next.

- **Support-ticket deflection and self-service rate.** This is the strongest dollar lever and the clearest before-and-after comparison. Read it from your helpdesk or support platform, such as Zendesk or Intercom. GitHub Issues or Discussions also work if that's where your users ask questions. After a Promptless-driven update to a documented topic, compare ticket volume on that topic before and after. The value is tickets deflected multiplied by your support team's fully loaded cost per ticket, a figure support ops usually has. Attribution is an estimate, so show it as a before-and-after comparison on a specific topic.
- **"Was this helpful?" ratings and feedback comments.** A per-page reader verdict, directly attributable to a specific updated page. That makes it ideal for before-and-after comparisons on a Promptless-driven change. It's often built into your docs platform, such as Mintlify, GitBook, or ReadMe, or added with a feedback widget. This signal is self-selected and skews negative, so read it as a trend and a triage signal, not an absolute score.
- **Site-search no-results rate and top queries.** The highest-signal map of content gaps, because users describe in their own words what they can't find. Read it from your site-search analytics, such as Algolia or your docs platform's search dashboard. Recurring no-result or high-volume queries show which gaps to close next and give you the language to match. A falling no-results rate on a topic after an update is a reader-side outcome.
- **Page views and engagement on changed pages.** Confirms reach: the audience for an updated page exists. Read it from your web or product analytics, such as GA4 or Plausible. Traffic alone isn't success, and a high bounce rate can be fine on a page that answers a question fast. Read engagement, such as GA4's engaged sessions or average engagement time, alongside raw views, and treat traffic as context, not proof.
- **Task completion rate.** The truest outcome: can readers do the thing? It also takes the most effort, since it needs usability testing or an instrumented funnel. Measure it when a topic is high-stakes enough to justify the effort.
- **Softer leading indicators.** Content freshness, doc coverage, and organic search traffic from a search console, such as Google Search Console, round out the picture. Frame any growth or conversion attribution as correlation only.

### Convert outcomes to a dollar value

If you have the cost inputs, put a dollar figure on the outcomes with these standard formulas:

- **Deflection value** = tickets deflected × fully loaded cost per ticket.
- **Contributor time saved** = doc updates shipped × hours saved per update × fully loaded hourly rate. "Doc updates shipped" is the same count you read from your Git history for the **Time saved** metric.
- **Composite ROI %** = (attributed value − program and tool cost) ÷ cost × 100.

Without cost inputs, the before-and-after trend carries the story: tickets or questions avoided, ratings climbing, or a falling no-results rate.

<Aside type="caution">
  Attribution is imperfect, because you can't prove a page view replaced a ticket. State every assumption you make, such as cost per ticket, hours per update, and deflection percentage. Prefer before-and-after and cohort comparisons over absolute claims, and present ranges over falsely precise figures.
</Aside>

## Build the case for leadership

Turn the signals into a short report you can repeat each period:

1. **Fix a period and a scope.** Choose a reporting window, usually a month or a quarter, and the doc collections it covers. Use the Triggers page **From** and **To** date fields to hold coverage numbers to that range. Read merged-PR dates from your Git history so every figure spans the same window.
2. **Pull the core counts.** Read merged Promptless PRs from your Git history, drafting and review activity from the Suggestions list, and coverage from the Triggers page for that window.
3. **Frame output as added capacity.** Lead with doc updates shipped and share of doc changes automated, the numbers that show the Docs function keeping pace with the product. Add time saved with your stated per-update assumption.
4. **Attach an outcome signal.** For the same collections and window, pull one or two outcome metrics from your own tools, such as ticket deflection, helpfulness ratings, or search gaps. The report then speaks to reader experience as well as throughput. Where you have the inputs, convert them to a dollar figure with the formulas above.
5. **Keep the sources visible.** Note where each number came from. Leadership trusts a figure it can trace to the Suggestions list, the Triggers page, or a merged PR far more than a bare total.
